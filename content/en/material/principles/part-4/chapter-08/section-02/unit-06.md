**IMFs involved:** Ion–dipole, hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking, van der Waals, electrostatic
**Why common:** Precipitation is a standard separation/purification method; crystallization is central to pharmaceutical, materials, and geological chemistry. Co-crystal formation is a major IMF-driven reaction class.

**Examples:**
- Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl(s) (precipitation from aqueous solution)
- Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄(s)
- Pharmaceutical co-crystals (e.g., carbamazepine–nicotinamide via H-bonding)
- Protein crystallization (hydrophobic + H-bonding + electrostatic)
- Salt formation (acid + base → ionic solid)
- Ice crystallization (H-bonding)
- Mineral precipitation (CaCO₃, etc.)

**Role of IMFs:** Desolvation and lattice formation are driven by H-bonding, ion–dipole, π–π, and van der Waals; co-crystals are held together entirely by IMFs.

## Why These Six?

- They span **aqueous, organic, biological, and materials chemistry**.
- They are **routinely taught** and **industrially/biologically pervasive**.
- In each, IMFs are **not incidental**—they determine mechanism, rate, selectivity, or assembly.
- Together they cover the major contexts where noncovalent interactions are mechanistically decisive: **proton transfer (1), substitution (2), addition (3), recognition/assembly (4), bond cleavage by solvent (5), and solid formation (6)**.

**Note:** Redox reactions, radical reactions, pericyclic reactions, and polymerizations also involve IMFs (solvent effects, pre-association), but the six above are the classes where IMFs are most commonly and centrally invoked in standard chemistry curricula and practice. -->
