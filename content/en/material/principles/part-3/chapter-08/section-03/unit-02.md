This is done through 4 steps:


1. **Evaporator (cold side) — heat absorption**

- Low-pressure liquid refrigerant enters and **boils** at low temperature.
 - It absorbs heat from the refrigerated space via **convection** (air/food → coil surface) and **conduction** through the coil wall, plus **latent heat of vaporization** inside.
 - This is the useful cooling: heat flows *into* the refrigerant because the refrigerant is colder than the load.

2. **Compressor — work input**
 - Vapor is compressed, raising **pressure and temperature** (so it's now hotter than ambient).
 - No heat transfer intended here, though some occurs (compressor shell loses heat).

3. **Condenser (hot side) — heat rejection**
 - Hot high-pressure vapor flows through coils; it **condenses** to liquid, releasing **latent heat** plus sensible heat.
 - Heat goes to the surroundings via **convection** to air (finned coils, fan) or to water (water-cooled systems), then conduction through the coil wall.
 - This is where the heat removed from the fridge (plus compressor work) is dumped.

4. **Expansion device (capillary tube / TXV) — pressure drop**
 - Refrigerant pressure and temperature drop (isenthalpic throttling), becoming cold liquid ready to absorb heat again.

**The energy balance**

Q_condenser = Q_evaporator + W_compressor

So the heat rejected to the room = heat removed from the food **plus** the work put in. That's why a fridge heats the room it's in.

**Heat-transfer mechanisms involved**

- **Conduction** — through coil walls, cabinet insulation, and food.
- **Convection** — natural or forced (fans) between air and coils; forced convection in most appliances.
- **Radiation** — minor in most domestic fridges (coil surfaces, cabinet exterior), more relevant in cold-storage/industrial settings.
- **Phase change (latent heat)** — the dominant heat-transfer mode at both coils: boiling in the evaporator, condensing in the condenser. This is what makes refrigeration efficient — large heat quantities per unit mass.

**Other refrigeration methods (same principle, different heat-transfer path)**

- **Absorption refrigeration** — heat (not compressor work) drives the cycle; uses a refrigerant/absorbent pair (e.g., ammonia–water, LiBr–water).
- **Peltier (thermoelectric)** — heat pumped by charge carriers; conduction + Joule/Peltier effects; low efficiency, small coolers.
- **Magnetic/adsorption refrigeration** — magnetocaloric or adsorption cycles.
- **Cryogenic methods** — Joule–Thomson expansion, Stirling, pulse-tube.

**Key design/performance factors tied to heat transfer**

- **Insulation** (conduction + radiation barriers) limits heat leak into the cold space.
- **Coil surface area and airflow** (convection) govern evaporator/condenser performance.
- **Temperature difference (ΔT)** across each coil drives heat transfer rate; too large a ΔT hurts efficiency (higher compressor work).
- **Fouling/ice buildup** adds conduction resistance on the evaporator — hence defrost cycles.
- **COP** (coefficient of performance) = Q_evap / W_comp; heat-transfer effectiveness directly sets achievable COP.

**Bottom line:** refrigeration is a **heat-pump cycle** — heat is absorbed at the evaporator (mostly by boiling refrigerant), carried by the refrigerant, and rejected at the condenser (by condensing), with a compressor providing the work to move heat "uphill" against the temperature gradient. Conduction, convection, and latent phase change are the transfer mechanisms; radiation is usually minor.
